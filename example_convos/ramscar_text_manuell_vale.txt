● That's exactly the right instinct, and it suggests a thesis structure rather
  than just a topic:

  Two-part design with a built-in safety net. Part 1 derives the
  information-theoretic predictions from existing corpus data — which gender the
  distributional account says a novel noun like Spezi should attract, and how
  confident that prediction is. Part 2 tests those predictions against real
  speakers in a rating study. Part 1 requires no participants at all, so if
  recruitment goes badly, ethics drags, or you get n=8 instead of n=30, you 
  still have a complete, defensible thesis — the empirical study becomes a
  smaller confirmatory chapter instead of the whole load-bearing wall. If
  recruitment goes well, you have a genuinely nice result.

  Supervisors tend to like this structure precisely because it de-risks the
  thing they'd otherwise worry about. And n≈20–40 is entirely normal for a
  bachelor's rating study — you're not underpowered for a within-items design
  where each participant rates many nouns, since your statistical power comes
  largely from the item count, not just the participant count.
